a field note on

Detecting Gravitational Waves

Einstein predicted them in 1916 and doubted anyone would ever measure them. It took 99 years and a machine sensitive to a fraction of a proton's width to prove him right.

1916

Einstein Predicts Them

General relativity implies massive accelerating objects should ripple spacetime itself — but Einstein thought the effect would be far too weak to ever detect.

1974

First Indirect Evidence

The Hulse–Taylor binary pulsar's orbit decays exactly as gravitational-wave emission would predict — a Nobel-winning clue, but not a direct detection.

1990s

LIGO Is Built

Twin L-shaped detectors, each arm 4 km long, built in the US to measure distortions smaller than 1/10,000th the width of a proton.

Sep 2015

First Direct Detection

LIGO catches the unmistakable "chirp" of two black holes, 1.3 billion light-years away, merging into one. Einstein was right all along.

2017

Nobel Prize & a New Astronomy

The detection earns the Nobel Prize in Physics. Months later, a neutron-star merger is seen in gravitational waves and light for the first time.

✎ The 2015 detection didn't just confirm a theory — it opened an entirely new way of observing the universe.
