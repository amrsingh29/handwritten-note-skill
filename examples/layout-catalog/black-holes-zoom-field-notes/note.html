Black Holes, at Three Zoom Levels

🔭 the 10-second version

A black hole is a place where gravity is so strong that nothing — not even light — can escape.

🔬 the 1-minute version

When a massive star runs out of fuel, it can collapse under its own gravity into an incredibly small, dense point. Past a certain distance from that point — the event horizon — the escape velocity needed to get away exceeds the speed of light. Since nothing travels faster than light, nothing that crosses that line ever comes back out. Not spaceships, not radio signals, not light itself.

That's why it's "black" — it doesn't emit or reflect anything. We only know it's there from how it bends light and pulls on nearby stars and gas.

🧪 the deep-dive

The event horizon's radius (the Schwarzschild radius) depends only on mass: r = 2GM/c². For the Sun, that works out to about 3 km — meaning if you crushed the Sun down to the size of a small town, it would become a black hole.

Inside, all that mass is thought to collapse to a singularity — a point of infinite density where our current physics stops making reliable predictions. Anything falling in gets stretched by the extreme tidal difference between the near and far side, a process physicists genuinely call spaghettification.

Black holes aren't perfectly black forever, either — Stephen Hawking showed they slowly emit Hawking radiation and, in principle, evaporate over timescales vastly longer than the current age of the universe. Whether the information that fell in is truly lost or preserved is the information paradox, still unresolved and still argued about. In 2019, the Event Horizon Telescope captured the first actual image of one, confirming the shadow-and-ring shape theory had predicted for decades.
