How a Flight Actually Works, Gate to Gate

Between the gate and the runway lights on the other end, a flight is really six distinct phases — each with its own job, its own risks, and its own crew choreography.

1 boarding

Loading the aircraft

Passengers, bags, and cargo get weighed and balanced — the plane's exact weight decides its takeoff speed and fuel burn.

weight & balance, checked twice
2 pushback & taxi

Getting to the runway

A tug pushes the plane back from the gate, then the crew taxis under their own power, guided by air traffic control the whole way.

often the slowest part of the trip
3 takeoff

The critical few minutes

Full thrust, a calculated speed to commit to flying, and a climb out — the most demanding phase for the crew, by far.

V1, Vr, V2 — the three speeds that matter
4 cruise

The calm middle

Autopilot holds altitude and course while the crew monitors fuel, weather, and traffic — the least eventful, longest phase.

35,000 ft, ~550 mph, mostly automated
5 descent & approach

Lining it up

A controlled descent, speed and flap changes, and lining up with the runway — often the busiest few minutes for the pilots.

stabilized approach, or go around
6 landing & taxi in

Back on the ground

Touchdown, reverse thrust and brakes to slow down, then one more taxi to the gate before the doors finally open.

the flight isn't over until the gate
✎ Six phases, one continuous handoff — nothing about a flight is really "just cruising."
